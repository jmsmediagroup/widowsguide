The Widow’s Guide · Book Seven

Year Two

Rebuilding a Life That Is Yours After Your Husband Dies

Months 15 to 24

You were half of something for decades. What are you now?

For some widows the second year is quieter, and stranger. The crisis has usually passed, and most of the urgent work is behind you. People may assume you are fine. Then a new question arrives: not how to get through the day, but what the days are for.

This book is about building a life that is yours, at your own pace, without pretending the last one did not happen and without living inside it. It suggests trying each change in a small version first, and it treats rest and staying put as choices too.

  • The small decisions that can carry a lot: the ring, your name, whose house it is
  • Work, volunteering, travel, moving: four things some widows try, and what to ask first
  • How work can affect Social Security benefits, and why Medicare premiums can change in year two
  • The home-sale tax rule and the date tied to his death
  • Guilt about feeling better, and why it can come with a good day
  • Keeping him with you: stories, recordings, rituals and gifts in his name
  • A one-page plan for the next ten years

The chapters are short, so you can read a little at a time. Each one opens with the question you are asking and gives the short answer first.

Tell me when it’s out

Inside: every chapter, and its short answer

Each chapter opens like this. The rest of it explains what exists, what to ask, and who can answer. It ends with what to do, what can wait, and where to find help.

1 Why does the second year feel so strange?

“Everyone thinks I'm fine now. I'm not sad the way I was. I'm just… lost.”

The second year can feel like this. The sharp grief can soften, many of the tasks may be done, and other people may think you are fine.

What is left can be quieter and harder to name. It is a life that no longer has its old shape, and does not yet have a new one.

Not every widow feels this, and the second year can also feel steadier than the first. If you do feel lost, it does not mean you have gone backward.

2 Who am I, if I'm not his wife?

“For forty years I was half of us. I don't know who I am on my own.”

You are still the person who was in that marriage. You are also someone you may not have met for a long time.

You can get to know her the way you find out anything: by noticing what you choose when nobody else is choosing with you. That takes time, and there is no date by which you need an answer.

3 Do I keep wearing the ring?

“I still wear my ring. Someone asked if I'm married and I didn't know what to say.”

Wear it, move it, put it away, or have it made into something else. None of these is a statement about how much you loved him, and none has a deadline.

The same is true of the other small decisions here: what to be called, what to say when asked, the photographs, his side of the bed. You can take them one at a time, in any order, or leave them all where they are.

4 Whose house is it now?

“His chair is still in the same place. Everything is arranged the way he liked it. It doesn't feel like mine.”

It is your home, and you are allowed to make it look that way. Changing a room does not erase him. He is in what you remember and what you choose to keep, wherever the sofa sits.

You can start with one room, or one corner, and see how it feels. You can go at whatever pace suits you, and stop whenever you like.

5 What do I actually want?

“People ask what I'm going to do now. I have no idea what I want.”

You don't have to know yet. Start small, and notice what catches your attention.

Wanting can come back slowly, often in small things first. When it does, work, volunteering, travel and moving are four things some widows try.

Each can be tried in a small version before you commit to anything. Resting, and staying where you are, are choices too.

6 Should I go back to work, or start something?

“I haven't worked in twenty years. Is it silly to think about a job now?”

It isn't silly. Work can bring money, a shape to the week, or company, and it can also mean starting something of your own.

Before you take paid work while you receive Social Security, ask Social Security how your earnings would affect your benefits. There can be a limit, and it can come as a surprise.

7 Would volunteering help?

“My friend says I should volunteer. I don't want to be one more lady in a hairnet.”

It can, if you find something that suits you. There is a lot more to choose from than hairnets.

Volunteering can bring back a role and a shape to the week, and it can put new people around you. It can also take more than you meant to give. One shift is a good place to start, before you promise anything regular.

8 Can I travel on my own?

“We always said we'd go to Italy. Can I go without him?”

Yes, you can, and some widows do. You can start with a short trip and choose the way of traveling that suits you: alone, with a friend, or in a group. Some moments may be hard.

The places you planned to see together can be the hardest to visit, and they can also be the best.

9 Should I move somewhere new?

“I've always wanted to live near the sea. Is it too late, or too soon?”

Neither, necessarily. A move in the second year can be a move toward something you want, and not only away from the grief.

A move still costs you neighbors, doctors and friends, so try the place before you commit. If you own your home, ask a tax preparer before you sell. The tax rule on selling has a date tied to his death.

10 Why do I feel guilty for feeling better?

“I laughed at dinner with friends and then felt terrible all the way home.”

Some widows describe exactly this. Guilt can come with a good day, because feeling better can feel like leaving him behind.

It isn't leaving him. Love doesn't need you to keep suffering to prove it.

11 How do I keep him with me?

“I'm afraid that if I build a new life, I'll forget him.”

You don't have to choose between him and a new life. Some widows keep their husbands with them through stories, rituals and things done in his name.

A new life can hold him. You can build one that is yours without pretending the last one did not happen and without living inside it.

12 What do I want the next ten years to look like?

“I've been thinking a month at a time. Should I be thinking further ahead?”

When you're ready, yes. A single page is enough.

It needs one line each for where you'll live, who you'll see, what you'll do, what the money allows, and what your papers say. A plan you can change is better than none.

Hear when Year Two is out

Five or six short emails a year at most, and a note when a new book is out. Each one is about something that tends to come up in the first two years. You can unsubscribe whenever you like.

For readers in the United States. We don’t sell or rent your address, and every email has a link to unsubscribe. How we handle your email

If today is very bad: call or text 988, or chat at chat.988lifeline.org, any hour. It is for anyone who is struggling; you do not have to be suicidal to reach out.

Veterans Crisis Line, also for family and friends: dial 988, then press 1, or text 838255. Crisis Text Line: text HOME to 741741. In an emergency, call 911.
