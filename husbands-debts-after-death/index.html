The money

Am I responsible for my husband’s debts?

Generally not. His debts are paid from his estate, with four exceptions worth checking.

Generally, no

His debts are paid from his estate, which is what he owned. If the estate can’t pay, the debt usually goes unpaid.

Being his executor means collectors can contact you about the estate. It does not mean you pay from your own money.

The four exceptions

  • A debt you co-signed.
  • An account you held jointly. Being an authorized user on his card is different from being a joint holder.
  • Living in a community property state, below.
  • A state law that makes a spouse pay certain necessary costs, such as health care.

If one of these may apply, ask a lawyer licensed in your state before you pay anything.

Community property states, including Texas and California

The community property states are Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington and Wisconsin. Alaska, South Dakota and Tennessee let couples opt in.

Living in one is among the exceptions. How it applies to a particular debt depends on that state’s law, so this is a question for a lawyer there.

His credit cards

If you were only an authorized user on his card, its debt is generally not yours. If you were a joint account holder, it may be.

Call each card company to stop new charges. You don’t have to discuss the balance on that call. You can say: I’m not discussing payment today. Please send me what you need in writing.

The free letter to card companies and lenders asks for the balance on the date he died, and whether you are a joint holder or an authorized user, in writing.

Medical bills and Medicaid

Some states make a spouse pay certain necessary costs, such as health care. Ask about your state before you pay one of his medical bills from your own money.

For people 55 or older, states must try to recover some Medicaid costs from their estate. They may not recover from the estate of someone survived by a spouse, a child under 21, or a blind or disabled child. Rules vary by state.

Student loans and the mortgage

Federal student loans are cancelled when the borrower dies, once the servicer gets proof of death. A Parent PLUS loan is cancelled if either the parent or the student dies.

The mortgage is one to keep paying if you can. If the home passed to you as a surviving joint owner, federal law generally stops a lender calling the whole loan due because he died. That covers a home of one to four units. More in what happens to the house.

When a collector calls or writes

A collector may discuss his debt with you as his surviving spouse, even if you aren’t the executor. That doesn’t make you liable.

Collectors may not say or hint that you must pay from your own money. They must give the debt’s details in the first conversation, or in writing within 5 days. If you dispute it in writing within 30 days, they must stop until they verify it.

The free letter to a debt collector disputes the debt and asks them to verify it.

Who can help

  • A probate or estate lawyer in your state. Your state or local bar association runs a referral service, found through the ABA directory.
  • Free civil legal aid for people on low incomes: lsc.gov.
  • Nonprofit credit counselors, through the National Foundation for Credit Counseling: nfcc.org, 800-388-2227.
  • The CFPB’s page on a relative’s debts: search “deceased relative’s debts” at consumerfinance.gov.
