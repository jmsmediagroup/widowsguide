For family and friends

What to say to a widow

What tends to help, what tends to land badly, and what to do when you can’t think of anything.

Say that he died, and say his name

Plain words are easier to hear than soft ones. “I’m so sorry Tom died” is kinder than a phrase that avoids it, because it doesn’t ask her to translate.

Widows often say the hardest thing is that people stop mentioning him at all. Using his name gives her permission to use it too.

Short is fine

You do not have to say the right thing, and there isn’t one. These are enough:

  • “I’m so sorry. I loved him too.”
  • “I don’t know what to say, but I’m here.”
  • “I was thinking about you today.”
  • “Tell me about him, if you want to.”

Then stop talking and let her answer, or not answer.

What tends to land badly

None of these come from unkindness, and they are all common. They ask her to feel something, or to be grateful, or to compare.

  • “He’s in a better place.” “Everything happens for a reason.” “God has a plan.”
  • “At least he didn’t suffer.” “At least you had so many years.” Anything starting “at least”.
  • “Be strong.” “You’re so strong.” She has no choice about it either way.
  • “I know exactly how you feel.” You might be close. You are not the same.
  • “Let me know if you need anything.” Well meant, and it puts the work on her.

Widows describe being told how to grieve as one of the things that wears them down most. What they say they want instead is facts, and permission.

Offer the specific thing, not the open door

“Let me know if you need anything” almost never gets used. A named offer does.

  1. “I’m going to the store on Thursday. What should I pick up?”
  2. “I’ll answer your phone for an hour so you can sleep.”
  3. “I’ll sit with you while you open the mail, and write down who you called.”
  4. “I’m bringing dinner Tuesday. I’ll leave it on the step if you’d rather not talk.”

An offer she can decline without explaining is the easiest kind to accept.

Then come back

The calls and the casseroles arrive in the first two weeks and then stop, often around the time the shock wears off. Some widows say the quiet that follows is harder than the funeral.

  • Call again in a month, and again in three.
  • Put his birthday, their anniversary and the day he died in your own calendar. Call the day before, not only the day itself.
  • Keep inviting her to things couples go to. The invitations are what dry up first.

If you’re not sure what she needs

Ask her plainly, and believe the answer. “Would you rather talk about him, or talk about something else?” is a real question with two good answers.

If she says she wants to be left alone today, that is information, not rejection. Come back next week anyway.
